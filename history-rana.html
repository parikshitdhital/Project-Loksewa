The Rana Regime

1846 – 1951 CE · Hereditary prime ministers · Kings as figureheads · 104 years

At a glance
  • Start: the Kot Parva of 1846, when Jung Bahadur seized power.
  • System: the prime minister, titled Shree Teen Maharaja, ruled; the Shah king was a figurehead. The post passed by a fixed roll of succession within the family.
  • Nine Rana prime ministers from Jung Bahadur to Mohan Shumsher.
  • Character: strong ties with the British, little schooling or press for ordinary people, and limited modernization.
  • End: the 1950–51 revolution and the Delhi settlement; 18 February 1951 (7 Falgun 2007) is celebrated as Democracy Day.

1. The Kot Parva and the rise of Jung Bahadur

After Bhimsen Thapa's fall, the court was torn by rival factions and a powerful queen, Rajya Laxmi. On the night of 14 September 1846 a nobleman was murdered, the court gathered at the Kot, and in the chaos more than 40 senior officials and nobles were killed. Jung Bahadur Kunwar, a rising army officer, emerged in control. He became prime minister, exiled the king's faction and, in 1847, set aside King Rajendra in favour of his son Surendra.

2. Jung Bahadur Rana (1846–1877)
  • Britain and Europe: his 1850–51 visit made him admire British power and law and strengthened ties with the Company.
  • Muluki Ain (1854): Nepal's first national legal code, which covered crimes, punishments and caste rules.
  • Schools: he opened the Durbar School in 1854, mainly for the elite.
  • Tibet: after a war in 1855–56, the Treaty of Thapathali gave Nepal annual payments and special trading rights.
  • 1857: he sent troops to help the British during the revolt; in 1860 Britain returned four western Tarai districts (Banke, Bardiya, Kailali, Kanchanpur), called Naya Muluk.
  • Hereditary rule (1856): his office, with the title Maharaja of Kaski and Lamjung, was made permanent in his family.

Transfers were often violent: Ranodip Singh was assassinated by his nephews in 1885, and Bir Shumsher's branch then ruled.

4. How the system worked
  • The king was kept secluded and had only ceremonial power; the prime minister signed and sealed orders.
  • Rana relatives held top army, civil and court posts. Rana sons were ranked A, B or C by their mother's status, which decided their rights to succeed.
  • Education for ordinary Nepalis, free press, and political parties were discouraged or banned.
  • Taxes and land grants (birta and jagir) kept the Ranas and their friends wealthy.
  • The palace complex of Singha Durbar showed the family's wealth and power.
5. Foreign policy: close to the British

The Ranas kept Nepal free of direct British rule by cooperating with it. Gurkha soldiers fought for Britain in both world wars. In the Treaty of 1923 Britain formally recognized Nepal as fully independent. After India became independent in 1947, Nepal signed the Treaty of Peace and Friendship with India in July 1950, shortly before the regime collapsed.

6. Reforms and modernization
  • Bir Shumsher: Bir Hospital (1889) and Kathmandu's first piped water supply.
  • Dev Shumsher (1901): opened schools, started the Gorkhapatra newspaper, and loosened controls, but was removed within months by his brother Chandra.
  • Chandra Shumsher: Tri-Chandra College (1918), Nepal's first hydropower plant at Pharping (1911), a short railway at the border (1927), and the Singha Durbar palace.
  • Social reform: sati was banned in 1920 and slavery abolished in 1924.
  • Padma Shumsher (1945–48): opened a girls' school (Padma Kanya) and issued a limited constitution in 1948, which was never really put into practice.
7. The first challenges (1930s–40s)

The earthquake of January 1934 hit the valley hard. Small groups of educated Nepalis began to push for change: Nepal Praja Parishad (1936) was the first political organization, led by Tanka Prasad Acharya and others. In 1941 the regime executed four of its members, known as the four martyrs: Shukraraj Shastri, Dharma Bhakta Mathema, Dasharath Chand and Gangalal Shrestha. Many Nepalis living in India, especially in Banaras and Calcutta, formed groups against the Ranas.

8. The end: the 1950–51 revolution
  • 1947: the Biratnagar jute mill strike became a symbol of resistance, and the Nepali Congress took shape in India.
  • April 1950: the Nepali National Congress and the Nepal Democratic Congress merged as the Nepali Congress.
  • 6 Nov 1950: King Tribhuvan and his family took refuge in the Indian embassy, then flew to Delhi. The Ranas crowned his grandson Gyanendra, but the move was not accepted abroad.
  • Armed uprising: the Congress's Mukti Sena fought in the hills and Tarai while talks went on.
  • Delhi settlement (Feb 1951): India helped broker a deal among the king, the Congress and the Ranas. Tribhuvan returned, and rule by the Ranas ended; Mohan Shumsher stayed on briefly as head of a mixed cabinet that included Congress leaders such as B. P. Koirala.

The date 7 Falgun 2007 BS (18 February 1951) is observed each year as Democracy Day.

9. Legacy

The Ranas left a few modern institutions, grand palaces, and a country that was largely cut off from the world. Illiteracy and poverty were widespread. The end of Rana rule opened the way to the first experiments with democracy, constitutions and elections.

Terms worth a closer look

Tap a dotted term anywhere above to see its meaning right there. This list is the same set, for reference.

Kot Parva

The "massacre at the Kot" of 1846, in which dozens of nobles and courtiers were killed in a single night. It ended the old court faction politics and brought Jung Bahadur to power.

Shree Teen Maharaja

The grand title of the Rana prime minister, written "Shree 3", to show he outranked all but the king, who was styled "Shree 5".

Roll of succession

A list of Rana brothers and cousins, in order of seniority and birth rank, that decided who would become the next prime minister. It was meant to avoid fights, but did not always work.

Muluki Ain

The "Law of the Land", issued in 1854, Nepal's first general legal code. It set rules on crime and property and also ranked castes, making social hierarchy part of law. Revised in 1963.

Treaty of Thapathali (1856)

The peace treaty after the Nepal–Tibet war of 1855–56. Tibet agreed to pay Nepal a yearly sum, and Nepal received trading rights and a representative in Lhasa.

Naya Muluk

The "New Country": four western Tarai districts (Banke, Bardiya, Kailali and Kanchanpur) returned by the British in 1860 as thanks for Nepal's help during the 1857 revolt.

Singha Durbar

The huge palace built by Chandra Shumsher in the early 1900s as the Rana prime minister's residence. Today it houses the main offices of Nepal's government.

Treaty of 1923

A treaty of peace and friendship with Britain in which Britain explicitly recognized Nepal as a fully independent state.

Four martyrs (Shahid)

Shukraraj Shastri, Dharma Bhakta Mathema, Dasharath Chand and Gangalal Shrestha, executed by the Rana government in 1941 for opposing the regime. Nepal marks Martyrs' Day in their memory.
