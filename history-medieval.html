Medieval Nepal — The Malla Period

879 – 1768 CE · Thakuri & early Malla · United valley · Three kingdoms

At a glance
  • Starts: 879 CE, when the Nepal Sambat era begins.
  • Core story: the Kathmandu Valley is united under Jayasthiti Malla (1382), then split into three kingdoms after 1482.
  • Society: a distinct Newar urban culture with codified rules and festivals.
  • Legacy: the three Durbar Squares, pagoda temples and the festival calendar of the valley.
  • Ends: 1768–69, when Prithvi Narayan Shah's Gorkha conquers the valley.

1. Sources and phases

Historians usually split this long span into: early medieval (879–c. 1200, Thakuri and local rulers), early Malla (c. 1200–1382, a time of rivalry and invasions) and later Malla (1382–1768, union then division).

Evidence comes from chronicles, copper-plate and stone inscriptions, manuscripts dated in Nepal Sambat, and the surviving temples and palaces themselves.

2. The early medieval period (879–c. 1200)

After the Licchavis faded, rulers often called Thakuri held power in parts of the valley, with the court shifting between towns. The new calendar, Nepal Sambat, was a sign of a fresh start.

To the south in Mithila, the Karnata dynasty was founded at Simraungadh around 1097 by Nanyadeva. Its later refugee king Harisimhadeva arrived in the valley in 1324, adding to its elite and cultural mix.

3. The rise of the Mallas (c. 1200–1382)

The name Malla appears as a royal title in the early 13th century with Arideva. The century that followed was harsh:

  • 1255: an earthquake killed Abhaya Malla and many of his people.
  • Raids from the west: the Khas Malla kings of the Karnali region attacked the valley several times from the late 1200s.
  • 1349: the Bengal sultan Shamsuddin Ilyas raided the valley and damaged shrines including Pashupati and Swayambhu.
  • Power was split between houses based at Bhaktapur, Kathmandu and Banepa, who often fought.
4. Jayasthiti Malla and the united valley (1382–1395)

Jayasthiti Malla married into the Bhaktapur house in 1354 and slowly gathered power, finally ruling the valley as one kingdom from 1382. He is remembered for order after chaos.

  • He is credited with organizing society into castes and occupations, and with a law code linked to the Nyayavikasini.
  • He standardized land measures and village duties, which helped the state collect revenue.
  • After his death in 1395 his sons ruled together; his grandson Yaksha Malla later expanded the kingdom.
5. Yaksha Malla and the division of 1482

Yaksha Malla (1428–1482) pushed Malla influence to the borders of the valley and into the Tarai and Tibetan routes. When he died, his sons split the realm. The traditional picture is three kingdoms, though a small Banepa kingdom existed briefly.

  • Kantipur (Kathmandu)
  • Bhaktapur (Bhadgaon)
  • Lalitpur (Patan), which became fully independent by the early 1600s

The rivalry led to wars over trade, rituals and small territory, but also to a race to build the grandest palaces and temples.

6. Famous Malla kings
  • Mahendra Malla (Kathmandu, 1560–1574): issued respected silver coins that circulated widely.
  • Siddhi Narasimha Malla (Patan, 1620–1661): a religious ruler who built the Krishna Mandir in 1637.
  • Pratap Malla (Kathmandu, 1641–1674): poet and scholar; had an inscription in many languages placed on the palace wall, built the Rani Pokhari, and promoted trade with Tibet.
  • Bhupatindra Malla (Bhaktapur, 1696–1722): great builder of the Nyatapola and the 55-window palace.
  • Jaya Prakash Malla (Kathmandu, 1736–1768): the last Malla king of Kathmandu; tradition links the Kumari custom with his reign.
  • Last rulers: Jaya Prakash (Kathmandu), Tej Narasimha (Patan), Ranajit (Bhaktapur).
7. Society and culture

The valley's Newar people built a rich urban culture. Buddhist and Hindu traditions mixed, with festivals like Indra Jatra and the chariot festival of Machhindranath. Rulers patronized drama, music, poetry and Nepal Bhasa literature. The Kumari tradition developed in this age.

8. Economy and trade

Prosperity rested on farming and trans-Himalayan trade between India and Tibet. Malla kings minted a coin called the mohar that was used widely in Tibet, which later became a source of conflict. Craft guilds, fine metalwork and woodcarving thrived.

9. Art and architecture
  • The three Durbar Squares (Kathmandu, Patan, Bhaktapur) are the best-known legacy.
  • The multi-tiered pagoda temple is a signature form of this age.
  • Skilled artists such as Araniko carried the Newar style abroad.
  • The valley was listed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site in 1979 (named seven monument zones).
10. Beyond the valley: west and hills

In the Karnali region the Khas Malla kingdom flourished and later split into smaller states. In the western hills, groups of small principalities known as the Baise and Chaubise rulers governed. The Sen kings held Palpa, Makwanpur and Vijayapur, and Mukunda Sen of Palpa even attacked the valley in 1526. In Gorkha, Drabya Shah (1559) founded the line that would one day unite Nepal.

11. Decline and conquest

The three kingdoms weakened one another and relied on shifting alliances. In 1767 Jaya Prakash Malla asked the British East India Company for help, but Captain Kinloch's expedition was defeated. In September 1768 Prithvi Narayan Shah entered Kathmandu during Indra Jatra; Patan fell soon after and Bhaktapur in 1769. This closes the Malla age and opens the unification era.

Terms worth a closer look

Tap a dotted term anywhere above to see its meaning right there. This list is the same set, for reference.

Nepal Sambat

A lunar calendar era beginning in 879 CE. It was long used for official records and is still used by the Newar community. Tradition links its start with the merchant Sankhadhar Sakhwa, who is said to have cleared the people's debts.

Newar

The indigenous urban community of the Kathmandu Valley. They speak Nepal Bhasa, practise both Hindu and Buddhist traditions, and built much of the art and architecture of the Malla age.

Chronicles (Vamsavalis)

Royal genealogies copied across the centuries. For the Malla period they are more reliable than for early times, but dates still need checking against inscriptions and manuscripts.

Araniko (Arniko)

A Newar artist and architect (1245–1306). He led a team of craftsmen to Tibet in the 1260s and then served at the court of Kublai Khan in China, where he is credited with the White Stupa in Beijing.

Karnata dynasty (Simraungadh)

A line of kings in Mithila, with its capital at Simraungadh in the southern plains, established around 1097. Its last king, Harisimhadeva, fled north in 1324 after an invasion, and his people strengthened the valley's elite.

Khas Malla kingdom

A kingdom in the Karnali basin of western Nepal, centred on Sinja in Jumla, that reached its height in the 13th and 14th centuries. Its kings sometimes raided the Kathmandu Valley and even visited Lumbini.

Nyayavikasini

A law text associated with Jayasthiti Malla's reign. It is used to describe the legal and social order he set up, including rules on caste and occupation.

Baise and Chaubise

The "22" and "24" principalities of the western hills, small kingdoms that held power before unification. Gorkha was among the Chaubise states.

Mohar (coin)

The silver coin issued by Malla kings, which was widely used in Tibet. Disputes over its quality and minting became a cause of later Nepal–Tibet conflict.

Pagoda temple

A temple with several stacked roofs, built in brick and carved wood. It is a Newar signature form, and its style later spread to China, Tibet and beyond.

Indra Jatra

An autumn festival in Kathmandu honouring Indra and the Kumari, with masked dances and chariot processions. Prithvi Narayan Shah's army entered the city while the festival was under way in 1768.

Kumari

The tradition of worshipping a young girl as a living form of the goddess Taleju. The Kathmandu custom is linked in tradition with the later Malla kings.
