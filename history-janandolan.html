The 1990 People's Movement

1990 CE (2046–47 BS) · Jana Andolan I · Multiparty democracy restored

At a glance
  • Launch: 18 February 1990 (7 Falgun 2046), led jointly by the Nepali Congress and the United Left Front.
  • Climax: huge protests on 6 April; on 8 April King Birendra lifts the ban on parties and ends the Panchayat system.
  • Interim government: sworn in on 19 April 1990 under Krishna Prasad Bhattarai.
  • New constitution: promulgated on 9 November 1990, creating a constitutional monarchy with a multiparty parliament.
  • First election: 12 May 1991; the Nepali Congress wins 110 of 205 seats.

1. Why 1990?
  • Tired of a partyless system: three decades of the king holding final power, banned parties, and limited free speech.
  • The 1989 India crisis: the border trade restrictions caused shortages and anger, weakening the government.
  • Events abroad: democracy movements in Eastern Europe in 1989 inspired the opposition and made repression harder.
  • A new alliance: the Nepali Congress and the left, long rivals, agreed to work together.
2. Who led it?
  • Nepali Congress: Ganesh Man Singh (the "supreme leader"), Krishna Prasad Bhattarai (party president) and Girija Prasad Koirala.
  • United Left Front (ULF): an alliance of communist groups, with leaders such as Sahana Pradhan and Man Mohan Adhikari.
  • Professionals and students: lawyers, doctors, engineers, teachers, writers and university students joined in large numbers.
  • Ordinary citizens: the movement was strongest in Kathmandu, Patan and Bhaktapur, but spread to many towns.
3. The movement, step by step

The movement was launched on 18 February 1990 (7 Falgun), the same date that Nepal marks as Democracy Day for 1951. The government answered with arrests, curfews and police action, but each crackdown brought more people out. Strikes spread, and the government lost control of the streets.

On 6 April (24 Chaitra) crowds in Kathmandu and Patan faced police firing. Many were killed, and the king dismissed the Panchayat cabinet. Two days later, on 8 April, he lifted the ban on parties, ending the Panchayat system after almost thirty years.

4. The interim government

Krishna Prasad Bhattarai led an 11-member interim cabinet with members from the Nepali Congress, the left, the king's nominees and independents. Its main tasks were to draft a new constitution and hold free elections. A Constitution Recommendation Commission, chaired by Justice Bishwanath Upadhyay, drafted the text. The palace published its own competing draft in October, but the interim government's version largely prevailed.

5. The 1990 constitution
  • Nepal was a constitutional monarchy with a multiparty parliamentary system; sovereignty was said to rest with the people.
  • A two-house parliament: a 205-seat House of Representatives and a National Council of 60 members.
  • Fundamental rights, including freedom of expression and association, and an independent Supreme Court.
  • Constitutional bodies such as the Election Commission, Public Service Commission, the Commission for the Investigation of Abuse of Authority (CIAA) and the Auditor General.
  • Nepal was declared a Hindu kingdom, and Nepali the national language, which minority groups criticised.
  • The king stayed head of state and supreme commander of the army, and kept emergency powers.
6. The 1991 election

On 12 May 1991, in the first multiparty elections since 1959, the Nepali Congress won 110 of 205 seats and the CPN (UML), a left merger, won 69. The interim prime minister, K. P. Bhattarai, lost his own seat, and Girija Prasad Koirala became prime minister on 29 May 1991. International observers described the vote as largely free and fair.

7. What it left unfinished
  • The monarchy kept real power over the army and emergency rule.
  • The Hindu-state and single-language provisions left many communities feeling excluded.
  • Governments changed often, and parties split.
  • Poverty and regional inequality continued, helping to fuel the Maoist insurgency that began in 1996.
8. Why it matters

The 1990 movement restored democracy and gave Nepal its first stable multiparty framework in decades. Its name, Jana Andolan I, distinguishes it from the second movement of 2006 (Jana Andolan II) that ended the monarchy's powers.

Terms worth a closer look

Tap a dotted term anywhere above to see its meaning right there. This list is the same set, for reference.

United Left Front (ULF)

An alliance of several communist groups that joined the Nepali Congress in the 1990 movement. Most of its groups later merged into the CPN (UML) in 1991.

Constitution Recommendation Commission

The nine-member body, with members chosen by the king, the Nepali Congress and the left front, that drafted the 1990 constitution under Justice Bishwanath Upadhyay.

Jana Andolan

"People's Movement". Nepal has had two: Jana Andolan I (1990) against the Panchayat system, and Jana Andolan II (2006) against the king's direct rule.

National Council (Rastriya Sabha)

The 60-member upper house under the 1990 constitution, partly elected by local bodies and the House of Representatives and partly nominated by the king.
