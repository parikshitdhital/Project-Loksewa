The 2015 Constitution & Federal Nepal

2015 CE (2072 BS) – present · Federalism · Elections · The 2025 youth uprising

At a glance
  • Promulgated: 20 September 2015 (3 Asoj 2072), observed as Constitution Day, after a 507–25 vote in the Constituent Assembly.
  • Structure: a federal democratic republic with three tiers: federal, 7 provinces and 753 local levels.
  • On this site: the full text (35 Parts, 308 Articles, 9 Schedules) is on the full-text page, with summaries on the overview page.
  • Since then: two amendments, local, provincial and federal elections, and many changes of government.
  • Latest turning point: the September 2025 youth protests, an interim government under Sushila Karki, and the March 2026 election.

1. The long road to a constitution

The 2008 Constituent Assembly had two years to write a constitution, but it repeatedly extended its term and ended in 2012 without one. The big disagreements were about how many federal states to create, how to draw their boundaries, and what kind of government to have. A second Assembly was elected in November 2013. The April 2015 earthquake added pressure to finish, and the leading parties struck a deal in June 2015.

2. Adoption and protest

On 16 September 2015, the 601-member Assembly passed the constitution 507 to 25. Parties from the southern plains and members from the Tharu community boycotted the process, saying their demands were ignored. Violent protests followed in the Tarai, with at least 40 deaths. The supply of goods through the Indian border was disrupted for months, causing shortages and a sharp debate about relations with India. On 20 September the president proclaimed the new constitution.

3. What the constitution sets up
  • State: a secular, inclusive, federal democratic republic.
  • Three tiers: the federal government; 7 provinces (Koshi, Madhesh, Bagmati, Gandaki, Lumbini, Karnali, Sudurpashchim); and 753 local levels.
  • Federal parliament: a House of Representatives of 275 members (165 directly elected, 110 by proportional representation) and a National Assembly of 59 (56 elected by provincial and local representatives, 3 nominated by the president).
  • Executive: a prime minister commanding a majority in the House; a largely ceremonial president elected by an electoral college.
  • Rights: a long list of 31 fundamental rights (Articles 16–46), including social and economic rights.
  • Inclusion: at least one-third of seats reserved for women at all three levels, and proportional inclusion for marginalised groups.
  • Independent bodies: the Election Commission, the anti-corruption commission (CIAA), the Auditor General, the Public Service Commission and many national commissions.
4. Amendments
  • First amendment (2016): made changes on representation and electoral constituencies after the protests.
  • Second amendment (18 June 2020): changed the official map of Nepal to include the Limpiyadhura–Lipulekh–Kalapani area, in a long-running border dispute with India.
  • Rules for amending the constitution are in Article 274; some basic features cannot be changed.
5. Putting federalism to work
  • 2017 elections: local elections in three phases (May to September), then provincial and federal elections in November–December.
  • Provincial governments, assemblies and chief ministers began work, and provinces chose their names.
  • 2022 elections: the second round, producing coalition governments again.
  • Presidents: Bidya Devi Bhandari (2015–2023), then Ram Chandra Paudel.
  • Prime ministers: frequent turnover; K. P. Sharma Oli held the post three times (2015–16, 2018–21, 2024–25), and Pushpa Kamal Dahal and Sher Bahadur Deuba also led governments.
6. The September 2025 youth uprising

The Gen Z protests began in early September 2025 the government had banned a number of social media platforms. Anger over the ban combined with long-standing frustration over corruption and nepotism, and young people, many in school uniforms, led huge protests on 8 September. Police fired on demonstrators, and at least 51 people were killed and more than 1,300 injured by early counts, one of the deadliest days of protest in Nepal's history.

  • On 9 September, parliament, the presidential residence and political offices were set on fire, and Prime Minister Oli resigned.
  • After talks among the president, the army chief and the protest leaders, former Chief Justice Sushila Karki was sworn in as interim prime minister on 12 September, the first woman to lead Nepal's government.
  • The president dissolved the House of Representatives and set elections for 5 March 2026.
7. The March 2026 election

The new Rastriya Swatantra Party (RSP) won a landslide on 5 March 2026, ending years of coalitions among the same older parties. Balendra "Balen" Shah, a former mayor of Kathmandu who became its prime ministerial candidate, took office on 27 March 2026 as the new prime minister, and the interim cabinet ended. His government faces expectations of cleaner administration, jobs and rebuilding trust in the system.

8. Debates that continue
  • Whether the three-tier federal system is working well and is affordable.
  • Representation and identity, especially for the Tarai, Dalit and Janajati communities.
  • Citizenship rules, which affect many families.
  • Corruption, youth unemployment and large-scale labour migration.
  • Whether constitutional changes are needed, and how to make them.
9. Why it matters

For the first time, Nepal's constitution was written by an elected Assembly rather than granted by a ruler. It sets the rules that today's exam questions on government, rights and federalism are based on, and it is the endpoint of the story that began with a handful of hill kingdoms.

Terms worth a closer look

Tap a dotted term anywhere above to see its meaning right there. This list is the same set, for reference.

753 local levels

The local governments that run daily services: 6 metropolitan cities, 11 sub-metropolitan cities, 276 municipalities and 460 rural municipalities. They are directly elected and have real powers over schools, health posts, roads and local taxes.

Proportional inclusion

The idea that public bodies and the state should reflect the diversity of the population. The constitution guarantees it for groups such as women, Dalits, Adibasi Janajati, Madhesis, Tharus and Muslims, and in state employment.

Constitution Day

Nepal's national day on 3 Asoj (20 September), marking the promulgation of the 2015 constitution.

Rastriya Swatantra Party (RSP)

A party formed in 2022 that grew from anti-establishment sentiment and went on to win the March 2026 election with Balendra Shah as its prime ministerial candidate.

Gen Z protests

The youth-led demonstrations of 8–9 September 2025 against a social-media ban, corruption and nepotism. They led to the prime minister's resignation and an interim government.
