The Unification of Nepal

1743 – 1816 CE · Prithvi Narayan Shah · "Greater Nepal" · The war with the British

At a glance
  • Founder: Prithvi Narayan Shah of Gorkha (born 11 Jan 1723, king from 1743, died 1775).
  • Turning point: the Kathmandu Valley falls on 25 September 1768; the capital moves to Kathmandu.
  • Peak: by about 1810 the state stretched from the Sutlej in the west to the Teesta in the east, often called Greater Nepal.
  • End: defeat in the Anglo-Nepal War and the Treaty of Sugauli (1815–16) fix the borders close to today's.
  • Remembered on: Prithvi Jayanti, 11 January, also called National Unity Day.

1. Gorkha before Prithvi Narayan Shah

Gorkha was a small hill kingdom founded by Drabya Shah in 1559, one of the Chaubise states north of the valley. It was poor in land but sat near the Kathmandu–Tibet trade routes. Prithvi Narayan Shah, a ninth-generation descendant of Drabya Shah, saw that control of those routes and the rich valley would make Gorkha strong.

2. The campaign step by step
  • Nuwakot (1744): the first big prize, cutting the valley's link to Tibet and the north.
  • Strategy of strangling: instead of attacking the three Malla kingdoms head-on, Gorkha cut their trade and supplies and slowly circled the valley.
  • Kirtipur (1767): a hard fight; the commander Kalu Pande died in an earlier attempt, and it fell on the third attack.
  • Sindhuli (1767): Jaya Prakash Malla asked the East India Company for help, but Captain Kinloch's force was beaten at Sindhuligadhi.
  • Kathmandu (1768): taken while the city celebrated Indra Jatra. Patan and then Bhaktapur (1769) followed.
  • East (to 1773): the Kirat hills and Vijayapur were absorbed, reaching toward the Koshi and beyond.
3. The Dibya Upadesh

Late in life Prithvi Narayan Shah gave his advice on running the state, collected as the Dibya Upadesh. Famous ideas include:

  • Nepal is a "yam between two boulders" (India and China), so it must be careful with both.
  • The country is a garden of four castes and thirty-six sub-castes, so all groups matter.
  • Protect trade and keep the economy strong; avoid dependence on outsiders.
  • Treat the army, officials and justice seriously and be wary of foreign influence.
4. After Prithvi Narayan Shah (1775–1806)

His son Pratap Singh Shah ruled briefly (1775–77), followed by Rana Bahadur Shah (1777–99), who came to the throne as a child. Queen Rajendra Laxmi and then Prince Bahadur Shah acted as regents and drove expansion.

  • West: Kumaon (1790) and Garhwal (1804) were taken, and Kangra was besieged until a Sikh army under Ranjit Singh ended the attempt (1809).
  • East: pressure on Sikkim.
  • Rana Bahadur Shah abdicated in 1799, lived in exile in Banaras (1801–04), returned, and was murdered in 1806.
  • A 1792 commerce treaty and the 1793 Kirkpatrick visit were early contacts with the Company; a 1801 treaty was weak and short-lived.
5. War with Tibet and China

Quarrels over the Nepali mohar coins, salt and trade led to war. In the first war (1788–89), a treaty favoured Nepal. In the second (1791–92) Gorkha troops plundered Tashilhunpo, so Tibet appealed to the Qing emperor. A large Chinese army advanced toward Kathmandu and forced the Treaty of Betrawati (1792). Nepal agreed to send a mission to Beijing every five years, a custom that lasted for over a century.

6. How the early state was run
  • The king was surrounded by the Bharadari council of nobles and officials; high officers were called Kaji.
  • Royal princes held the rank of Chautaria and acted as senior advisers or regents.
  • Land grants (birta for service or religion, jagir for officials) rewarded loyalty.
  • The army was the backbone of the state and recruited from many hill groups.
  • Palace politics were fierce, and factions often fought over regencies.
7. Bhimsen Thapa (1806–1837)

After Rana Bahadur Shah's murder, Bhimsen Thapa took control as Mukhtiyar under the child-king Girvan Yuddha. He modernized the army, built the Dharahara tower (1832), and tried to keep the British at bay. He fell in 1837 and died in 1839.

8. The Anglo-Nepal War (1814–1816)

Causes: border quarrels in the Tarai (Butwal and Sheoraj), British wish for trade routes to Tibet and Central Asia, and Nepal's ambitions. War was declared on 1 November 1814.

  • Nalapani/Kalunga fort (1814): about 600 defenders under Balabhadra Kunwar held off a larger force, killing the British general Gillespie, before withdrawing.
  • West: Amar Singh Thapa lost ground and surrendered at Malaun in May 1815; Bhakti Thapa also fell in the western fighting.
  • Makwanpur (Feb 1816): a British advance toward Kathmandu forced Nepal to ratify the peace.

Terms of the Treaty of Sugauli included: Nepal gave up Sikkim, Kumaon, Garhwal and much of the Tarai (parts later returned); accepted a British Resident in Kathmandu; and its borders were set roughly at the Mechi in the east and the Mahakali in the west. Gurkha recruitment into British service began soon after.

9. Why it matters

Unification created one state from dozens of principalities and gave Nepal its identity and capital. The war with Britain ended expansion but preserved Nepal's independence, unlike most of South Asia. The border and the habit of careful balancing between neighbours trace back to this era.

Terms worth a closer look

Tap a dotted term anywhere above to see its meaning right there. This list is the same set, for reference.

Greater Nepal

The name given to Nepal at its widest extent, around 1810, when it reached from the Sutlej river in the west to the Teesta in the east. Sugauli later reduced it to roughly today's shape.

Treaty of Sugauli

The 1815–16 agreement that ended the Anglo-Nepal War. Nepal lost large territories, accepted a British Resident, and kept its independence. It is one of the most cited treaties in Nepali history.

Treaty of Betrawati (1792)

The peace settlement that ended the second Nepal–Tibet war after Qing China intervened. Nepal agreed to send missions to Beijing every five years and to respect Tibet.

Dibya Upadesh

The "divine counsel" of Prithvi Narayan Shah, a set of teachings on government, trade, defence and foreign relations, recorded by those who heard him. It remains a key text for understanding early Nepali statecraft.

Kalu Pande

The Gorkha commander (Kaji) who led the army in the early Kirtipur battles. He was killed in 1757 and is remembered as a hero of the unification wars.

Bharadari

The royal council of senior nobles and officials who advised the Shah kings. The word "Bharadar" later came to mean an official of the court.

Kaji

A high-ranking minister or commander in the Shah court, often from leading families. Several Kajis served as military leaders during the unification.

Chautaria

A title for princes of the royal blood who held senior positions at court, including regencies.

Mukhtiyar

The chief minister, who often ruled in the king's name when the king was a child. Bhimsen Thapa held the post for over thirty years.

Bhimsen Thapa

The most powerful minister of the early nineteenth century. He led Nepal into the war with Britain, strengthened the army, and fell from power in 1837 after palace intrigue.

Balabhadra Kunwar

The commander who defended the fort at Nalapani (Kalunga) in 1814. His defence, and the respect it earned from the British, is an enduring symbol of Gorkhali courage.
