The Panchayat Era

1960 – 1990 CE · Partyless system · Kings Mahendra and Birendra · 30 years

At a glance
  • Start: King Mahendra's coup on 15 December 1960 (1 Poush 2017 BS) ends Nepal's first elected government.
  • System: a four-tier, partyless Panchayat under the 1962 constitution, with the king holding final authority.
  • Mahendra (to 1972) built roads and reformed law and land; Birendra (1972–2001) faced rising demands for democracy.
  • Turning point: the 1980 referendum narrowly kept the system (about 55%).
  • End: the 1990 People's Movement; the ban on parties is lifted in April 1990.

1. Before the coup: Nepal's first elections

King Mahendra, who took the throne after his father Tribhuvan died in 1955, issued the 1959 constitution. In the 1959 elections the Nepali Congress won a clear majority and B. P. Koirala became prime minister. His government pursued land and administrative reform, which upset the palace, landowners and the army.

2. The coup of 1960

On 15 December 1960 Mahendra dismissed Koirala's cabinet, dissolved parliament, banned political parties and arrested leaders including Koirala. He said parties had failed and that Nepal needed a system "suited to its soil". In January 1961 he introduced the Panchayat system. Many Congress leaders went into exile in India, from where they ran a long resistance.

3. The 1962 constitution and the structure

The constitution of 16 December 1962 created a pyramid with the king on top:

  • Village (and town) panchayats elected directly by the people.
  • District panchayats chosen by those below.
  • Zonal (anchal) assemblies, fourteen in number, which acted as electoral colleges.
  • Rastriya Panchayat, the national assembly in Kathmandu, indirectly elected, with some members nominated.

Political parties were banned. Official class organizations (for youth, women, peasants, workers, ex-servicemen and others) were created to channel participation. The king named the prime minister, could rule by decree, and was the source of sovereign power. The constitution was amended in 1967, 1975 and 1980.

4. Mahendra's policies (to 1972)
  • Law: the 1963 Muluki Ain ended legal caste discrimination, ending the rank-based rules of the 1854 code.
  • Land: the 1964 Land Reform put limits on land holdings and tried to protect tenants, though implementation was uneven.
  • Administration: the country was reorganized into 14 zones and 75 districts (1962); five development regions came in 1972.
  • Roads and infrastructure: the East–West highway began, and the Kathmandu–Kodari road to the Chinese border opened in the late 1960s.
  • Nationalism: the slogan "one language, one dress, one nation" promoted the Nepali language and a single national identity, which minority groups later criticised.
  • Foreign policy: treaties with China (1960–61, including the boundary agreement) balanced the close ties with India.
  • Back to the Village: a national campaign (from 1967) to push development to villages and strengthen loyalty to the system.
5. Birendra and the Zone of Peace (1972–)

Mahendra died in January 1972, and his Eton- and Harvard-educated son Birendra took over. At his coronation in February 1975, Birendra proposed that Nepal be a Zone of Peace, a neutral buffer between India and China, which many countries endorsed. The 1971 National Education System Plan expanded schools, and the panchayat system tried to show it could deliver development.

6. Opposition and resistance
  • Nepali Congress: its leaders were jailed or in exile; B. P. Koirala was imprisoned for years, lived in India, and was arrested again when he returned in December 1976.
  • Communist groups split into many factions; the 1971 Jhapa uprising was an early armed revolt.
  • Students: in 1979, protests that began over a student issue and the execution of Pakistan's Bhutto grew into a demand for democracy.
7. The 1980 referendum

Under the pressure of the 1979 protests, King Birendra called a national vote on 2 May 1980: keep the Panchayat system with reforms, or return to a multiparty system. The reformed Panchayat won narrowly, about 55%, though critics said the vote was unfair. The third amendment (December 1980) allowed direct elections to the national assembly and made the prime minister answerable to it.

8. The final decade (1980–1990)
  • Elections were held in 1981 and 1986; the Congress boycotted the 1986 polls.
  • 1985: the Nepali Congress launched a satyagraha, and bomb blasts shook Kathmandu.
  • Corruption and inequality grew, and the economy struggled.
  • 1989: India–Nepal trade and transit treaties expired, and a tense dispute (including Nepal's purchase of Chinese arms) led to border restrictions that hurt Nepal's economy.
  • In 1990, a joint movement by the Congress and the left led to 18 February 1990 and the end of the system (next era).
9. Legacy

The Panchayat period built roads, schools and national institutions and gave Nepal a strong sense of unity, but it centralised power in the palace, limited free speech and sidelined many communities. Debates over the monarchy's role, language and identity all trace back to this period.

Terms worth a closer look

Tap a dotted term anywhere above to see its meaning right there. This list is the same set, for reference.

Panchayat system

A "partyless" system of councils, from village to national level, under the king. Supporters called it a democracy suited to Nepali traditions; critics saw it as royal autocracy.

Class organizations

State-sanctioned bodies for groups such as youth, women, peasants, workers and ex-servicemen. They stood in for political parties and were meant to support the system.

Land Reform Act, 1964

A law that limited the size of landholdings and gave tenants some rights. It was an important change, but landlords found ways around many rules.

Back to the Village National Campaign

A campaign launched in 1967 that sent officials and students to villages to promote development and loyalty. It was later turned into a political tool and suspended in 1979.

Zone of Peace

King Birendra's 1975 proposal to declare Nepal a neutral zone of peace between India and China. Many countries backed it, but India never formally accepted it.

1980 referendum

The vote on 2 May 1980 offering a choice between a multiparty system and a reformed Panchayat. The reformed Panchayat won about 55%, but opponents said the process was unfair.
