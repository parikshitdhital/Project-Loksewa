The 1951 Revolution

1950 – 1951 CE (2007 BS) · The fall of the Ranas · Return of the Shah monarchy

At a glance
  • Trigger: King Tribhuvan takes refuge in the Indian embassy on 6 November 1950.
  • Armed wing: the Nepali Congress's Mukti Sena attacks in the Tarai from 11 November 1950.
  • Settlement: the Delhi settlement, mediated by India, ends Rana rule in February 1951.
  • Date to remember: 7 Falgun 2007 BS (18 February 1951), observed as Democracy Day.
  • Aftermath: an interim constitution, shaky coalition governments, and a promised Constituent Assembly that was not held for decades.

1. Why the Rana system was weakening
  • India's independence (1947) removed the Ranas' main protector, the British. The new Indian government favoured reform in Nepal.
  • Returning soldiers from World War II brought new ideas about freedom and the wider world.
  • Educated Nepalis, many studying in India, formed groups in Banaras, Calcutta and elsewhere.
  • Divisions among the Ranas: the "B" and "C" class Ranas had fewer rights, and some joined the opposition.
  • The king: Tribhuvan, kept almost powerless, quietly supported the reformers.
2. The forces and leaders
  • Nepali Congress: led by B. P. Koirala (the main strategist), his half-brother Matrika Prasad Koirala (the party's president), Subarna Shumsher (a Rana who joined the movement and helped run the armed wing) and Ganesh Man Singh (an organizer).
  • Tanka Prasad Acharya of the older Praja Parishad and the Nepali National Congress.
  • Communists: the Communist Party of Nepal was founded in Calcutta in 1949 under Pushpa Lal Shrestha, and some members took part.
  • King Tribhuvan: the symbol of the revolution.
  • India: under Prime Minister Nehru, it sheltered the king and mediated, wanting a stable, friendly Nepal.
3. The road to November 1950

In 1946–47 the Nepali National Congress was set up in India and spread into Nepal. The Biratnagar jute mill strike of 1947 became a first major protest, and B. P. Koirala was arrested. Padma Shumsher's reform offer in 1948 was weak, and his hard-line successor Mohan Shumsher banned the party. In March–April 1950 the Congress joined with the Nepal Democratic Congress to form the Nepali Congress, which decided to take up arms.

4. The revolution
  • 6 Nov 1950: Tribhuvan slipped away from the palace on the pretext of a hunting trip and reached the Indian embassy, a bold step that shocked the regime.
  • 7 Nov: Mohan Shumsher put the four-year-old Gyanendra on the throne; India and most countries refused to recognize him.
  • 10 Nov: Indian planes flew the royal family to Delhi, leaving the infant behind.
  • 11 Nov: the Mukti Sena struck in the Tarai and spread to the eastern and western hills; Congress fighters came to control several districts, especially in the east. The Rana army struggled to hold the countryside.
  • Protests spread to Kathmandu and the valley towns, and international pressure grew.
5. The Delhi settlement

With the fighting dragging on and India offering mediation, representatives of the king, the Congress and the Ranas met in Delhi. They agreed on a compromise (often called the Delhi Compromise or Delhi Accord):

  • An end to Rana autocracy; the king regained real power.
  • A joint interim cabinet of five Ranas and five Nepali Congress members, headed by Mohan Shumsher.
  • A promise to elect a Constituent Assembly to write a constitution.

Tribhuvan returned in mid-February 1951, and 18 February (7 Falgun) is celebrated as Democracy Day. Some Congress leaders, including K. I. Singh and Ram Prasad Rai, rejected the deal.

6. The interim constitution

The Interim Government of Nepal Act, 1951, drafted with help from Indian experts, replaced the Rana-era system. It created a cabinet responsible to the king, guaranteed some rights, and set up a basic administrative structure until an elected assembly could write a permanent constitution.

7. The unsteady years (1951–1955)
  • Nov 1951: the Congress ministers resigned, ending the Rana–Congress cabinet. On 16 November the king appointed Matrika Prasad Koirala as prime minister, the first commoner to hold the post.
  • Jan 1952: the Raksha Dal revolt led by K. I. Singh was put down, and he fled the country.
  • Koirala resigned in August 1952. Governments rose and fell, and the king ruled directly for periods.
  • The promised Constituent Assembly was never held. Political parties and the palace argued over who should rule.
  • 13 March 1955: King Tribhuvan died; his son Mahendra became king. Nepal joined the United Nations in December 1955.
8. Why it matters

The revolution ended 104 years of family rule, opened Nepal to the world, and began the era of political parties, schools and development plans. It also set patterns that lasted: a strong monarchy, an influential India, and deep disagreements over how to write a constitution. Many of these themes returned in 1960, 1990 and 2006.

Terms worth a closer look

Tap a dotted term anywhere above to see its meaning right there. This list is the same set, for reference.

Mukti Sena

The "Liberation Army", the armed wing of the Nepali Congress. Its fighters attacked Rana posts and held towns in the Tarai and hills in late 1950, forcing the Ranas to the table.

Nepali Congress

The political party formed in 1950 by merging the Nepali National Congress and the Nepal Democratic Congress. It led the movement against the Ranas and later won Nepal's first elections in 1959.

Delhi Compromise / Accord

The early 1951 agreement among King Tribhuvan, the Nepali Congress and the Rana government, mediated by India. It ended Rana autocracy and created an interim joint cabinet.

Interim Government of Nepal Act, 1951

The first interim constitution after the Ranas. It gave a basic framework for government, with the king as head of state and a cabinet to run the administration.

Constituent Assembly

An elected body whose job is to write a new constitution. It was promised in 1951 but Nepal did not elect one until 2008.

Raksha Dal

The home guard force of former Mukti Sena fighters. Disagreement about their future and the Delhi deal led to an unsuccessful revolt in January 1952.
