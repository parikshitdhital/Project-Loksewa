The Maoist Insurgency

1996 – 2006 CE (2052–2063 BS) · The "People's War" · A decade of armed conflict

At a glance
  • Start: 13 February 1996 (1 Falgun 2052), when the CPN (Maoist) launched its People's War with simultaneous attacks in six districts.
  • Aim: to abolish the monarchy and create a people's republic, with deep social and economic change.
  • Human cost: about 17,000 people killed by most counts (earlier estimates were lower), and thousands of disappearance cases.
  • Turning points: the 2001 royal massacre, the November 2001 emergency, and the 1 February 2005 royal takeover.
  • End: the Comprehensive Peace Accord of 21 November 2006.

1. Why did it happen?

Researchers point to a mix of causes rather than a single one:

  • Poverty and inequality: especially in the remote mid-western hills, with little land, few roads and weak services.
  • Exclusion: many Dalits, Janajati and Madhesi communities, and women, felt shut out of power and jobs.
  • Disappointment with 1990: multiparty democracy brought freedom, but frequent government changes and corruption left many people feeling that daily life had not improved.
  • Political ideology: a Maoist movement, inspired by China's Mao Zedong, that wanted to end the monarchy and "feudal" structures.
2. The Maoists and their demands

The Maoist party, led by Pushpa Kamal Dahal "Prachanda", with Baburam Bhattarai as its main ideologue, grew from a split in the communist movement. In February 1996 it sent a 40-point list of demands to the government, including a republic, a new constitution made by an elected assembly, rights for ethnic groups and women, and an end to unequal treaties. After the deadline passed, the war began. Maoist areas ran parallel "people's governments", and their fighting force became the People's Liberation Army.

3. Phase one: police operations (1996–2001)

At first the government treated the rebellion as a police matter. A police crackdown in Rolpa in 1995, known as Operation Romeo, and later operations such as Kilo Sierra Two (1998), were criticised for abuses and helped push villagers toward the rebels. The Maoists steadily gained control over rural areas in the mid-west, though the army stayed in barracks and the government held the main towns.

4. Royal massacre and the emergency (2001–2003)
  • 1 June 2001: King Birendra and most of his family were killed in the palace; Crown Prince Dipendra was named as the shooter in the official inquiry, and Gyanendra became king.
  • Ceasefire and talks (July–November 2001) failed. On 23 November the Maoists struck widely.
  • 26 November 2001: the government declared an emergency and, for the first time, sent in the Royal Nepali Army. Violence rose sharply.
  • An Armed Police Force was created in 2002 to fight the rebels.
  • In 2003, a second ceasefire lasted only a few months.
5. The king takes control (2002–2005)

In October 2002 King Gyanendra dismissed the elected prime minister, and for the next years appointed governments himself. On 1 February 2005 he dissolved the government, declared a state of emergency and took direct control, promising to end the insurgency. Instead, political parties and the Maoists moved closer together against him.

6. The human cost
  • About 17,000 people died by most recent counts; earlier estimates were around 13,000.
  • More than 3,000 enforced-disappearance cases were reported.
  • Hundreds of thousands were displaced, and many schools, bridges and offices were damaged.
  • Both sides were accused of serious abuses, including killings of civilians, torture, abductions and, in the Maoist case, recruiting children.

Numbers vary by source because records were incomplete.

7. The road to peace (2005–2006)
  • November 2005: the Seven-Party Alliance and the Maoists signed a 12-point understanding in Delhi: parties would fight for democracy and the Maoists would accept multiparty politics.
  • April 2006: the second People's Movement filled the streets. The king reinstated parliament on 24 April.
  • Ceasefire and talks followed, with a request to the United Nations to monitor arms (August 2006).
  • 21 November 2006: the Comprehensive Peace Accord ended the war.
8. After the war
  • The UN Mission in Nepal (UNMIN) started work in January 2007 to monitor the arms of both sides.
  • An Interim Constitution came into force on 15 January 2007, and a Constituent Assembly election followed in 2008.
  • Maoist fighters moved into cantonments; some were later integrated into the Nepal Army and others were given payments.
  • A Truth and Reconciliation Commission and a Commission on Enforced Disappearances were created in 2015, but many cases remain unresolved.
9. Why it is still debated

Nepalis hold different views of the conflict: as a violent crisis that hurt many ordinary people, as a driver of inclusion and the republic, or both. Historians generally agree it reshaped politics, ended the monarchy's power, and put social inclusion at the centre of the national debate.

Terms worth a closer look

Tap a dotted term anywhere above to see its meaning right there. This list is the same set, for reference.

CPN (Maoist)

The Communist Party of Nepal (Maoist), formed in the mid-1990s, which led the armed struggle. It later entered mainstream politics and became the largest party in the first Constituent Assembly in 2008.

People's War

The Maoists' name for their armed campaign, based on the idea of a long rural struggle that surrounds the cities. The government called it an insurgency.

40-point demands

A list sent to Prime Minister Sher Bahadur Deuba on 4 February 1996. It asked for a republic, a new constitution, an end to discrimination, and changes in land, trade and treaty policy. The war began after the 15-day deadline passed.

12-point understanding

The November 2005 agreement in Delhi between the Seven-Party Alliance and the Maoists. It laid the base for the 2006 movement and the peace process.

Comprehensive Peace Accord (CPA)

The agreement signed on 21 November 2006 by the government and the Maoists. It ended the war, set up arms management, and promised an elected Constituent Assembly.

UNMIN

The United Nations Mission in Nepal, set up in 2007 at the request of both sides to monitor weapons and armies and help with elections. It closed in 2011.
